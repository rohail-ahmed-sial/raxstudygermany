Germany visa guide

Every main route to Germany explained in plain language, with official sources, checklists and planning tools.

Last checked: 29 October 2026 Verified

Germany has a separate visa route for each main purpose of stay: studying, learning German, vocational training, work, looking for work, visiting and joining family. Each route has its own conditions, money rules and documents. These guides explain them in plain language and link every changeable figure to the official page we checked.

This is a plain-language summary to help you prepare. The German mission responsible for you decides what it needs, and its latest checklist always comes first. Use the route finder if you are not sure where to start.

Short stay or long stay?

A Schengen visa (type C) is for stays of up to 90 days in any 180-day period, for example tourism, visiting family or a business trip. A national visa (type D) is for longer stays with a specific purpose such as study, training or work. After you arrive with a national visa, you apply for a residence permit at the foreigners’ authority (Ausländerbehörde) where you live.

Language certificates and IELTS

No single certificate is needed for every visa. Some routes name a German level (for example A1 for many spouses, A2 for a recognition partnership, B1 for vocational training). Universities and employers set their own language rules, which may be German or English. See which German level each route names and when IELTS matters.

How this guide is checked

Every changeable figure on these pages (fees, amounts of money, salary thresholds) is stored with its source, the date we checked it and a status. When we could not confirm a figure on an official page, the site says Verify current requirement instead of guessing. The site does not update itself: amounts often change on 1 January, so always compare with the official page before you apply.
